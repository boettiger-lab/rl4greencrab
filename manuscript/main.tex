As it’s increasingly used, we’re presented with two challenges: right-sizing model complexity and interpreting the results
1. When does model complexity and inclusion of biological + observation process realism matter in decision-making? It's appealing from a biological/ecological inference perspective to include as much biological realism as possible, and from a decision-making perspective, more realism can mean more realistic (and better) actions. But at a certain point, this biological/observation realism likely introduces noise into the solution. RL can help us iterate on model/MDP complexity to understand this trade-off/boundary.
2. Continuous MDPs are traditionally computationally intractable to solve, hence why we need approximation approaches. One classic solution is to discretize the problem (i.e., state, action, or belief space) before it is solved. But this can be problematic because these discretizations can be made arbitrarily and we want to find solutions given the entire space of the problem. However, continuous/high-dimensional solutions can be difficult to interpret and/or not actionable. Here we want to discretize the solution space, rather than the problem space

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Markov decision processes}

Markov decision processes (MDPs) are useful for modeling sequential decision-making problems under uncertainty in stochastic systems \parencite{bellman1957markovian}. MDPs are described by a tuple $<S, A, P, r, T>$, where $S$ is the state space representing possible configurations of the system, $A$ is the set of all possible actions that control the state dynamics, $P$ describes the state dynamics of the system (i.e., $P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)$ representing the probability of transitioning to state $s_{t+1}$, given the action $a_t$ is applied to state $s_t$), $r$ is the reward as a function of the state and action (i.e., $r(s_t, a_t)$), and $T$ is the time horizon over which actions are implemented. Desirable actions and states receive higher rewards. If the state space $S$ is imperfectly observed, the MDP becomes a partially observable Markov decision process (PoMDP), where the above tuple now contains $O$ and $Z$. Here, $O$ is the set of possible observations of the state, and $Z$ is the observation function (i.e., $Z(o_{t+1}|a_t,s_{t+1})$ representing the probability of observing $o_{t+1}$, given that action $a_t$ led to state $s_{t+1}$) \parencite{chades2021primer}.

Markov decision processes are often solved exactly using methods like stochastic dynamic programming \parencite{marescot2013complex}, finding an optimal policy $\pi^*: O \rightarrow A$ that maps a decision (i.e., action) to each possible observation. In contrast, reinforcement learning (RL) approximates this policy function, yet has few restrictions on what comprises a state or action in that they may be continuous or discrete and single- or multi-dimensional, for example. Briefly, RL is comprised of two components: an environment and an agent (Figure 1). The agent receives an observation $o_t$ of the environment and performs an action $a_t$ that causes the environment to transition to a new state and return a reward $r$ to the agent. The goal of the agent is to learn the optimal policy $\pi^*$ ($a_t|o_{t-1}$) that maximizes its expected cumulative reward. A more extensive description of deep reinforcement learning can be found in \textcite{lapeyrolerie2022deep}. 

\subsection{European green crab biology and management}

European green crab (\textit{Carcinus maenas}) population dynamics are characterized by complex, size-structured demography and stochastic recruitment events \parencite{keller2026integrated}. Natural mortality varies as a function of size, due to strong negative intra-specific adult-juvenile interactions like cannibalism \parencite{grosholz2021stage, romano2017cannibalism}, size-structured competition with native crabs \parencite{mcdonald2001competitive, jensen2007biotic}, and uneven vulnerability to thermal stress and starvation \parencite{hurst2007causes, henderson1988size}. The rate of growth in body size is also size-dependent, with smaller crabs molting at a much higher frequency than larger crabs \parencite{yamada2005growth}. Additionally, green crab population dynamics are driven not only by local reproduction but also strongly by regional-scale processes, and rates of colonization can vary by orders of magnitude from year to year \parencite{yamada2021ocean} (Figure 2A). While green crabs occupy sheltered bays and estuaries as adults, planktonic larvae enter the open ocean and can travel hundreds of kilometers from their source during certain oceanographic conditions like El Niño \parencite{du2024dispersal, engel2025larval}. 

Both observation and control of a green crab population occurs through removal trapping, such that the actions of managers both alter the state dynamics and generate observations. The population is also partially observable, as these trap observations are not only imperfect measurements of adult abundance, but the size-selectivity of the traps leaves all size classes below a certain threshold completely unobserved \parencite{keller2026integrated}. Due to this size-selective trapping, as the population changes in size structure over time, the availability for removal and knowledge about the system also change over time (Figure 2B). Importantly, size-selective removal can have consequential interactions with the size-structured demography of green crabs; removal of larger crabs can increase the survival of juveniles, also known as overcompensation or the ``hydra effect", as demonstrated by an eradication program that resulted in an increase in population abundance in response to removal \parencite{grosholz2021stage}.

\subsection{PoMDP formulation}

Here we describe how the green crab decision problem is represented as a partially observable Markov decision process (PoMDP). We adapt a size-structured population dynamics and removal model developed in \cite{keller2026integrated} to describe the state dynamics, $P(\textbf{s}_{t+1}|\textbf{s}_t,a_t, M(t))$. In the context of reinforcement learning, the state dynamics, $P$, are synonymous with the RL environment with which an RL agent interacts (Figure 1). Here, the state, $\textbf{s}_t$, is a vector of size-structured abundance discretized into 22 size bins. The population changes through time due to seasonal growth in body size, natural mortality, removal, and annual recruitment. Natural mortality, harvest mortality, and the abundance of local and non-local recruits entering the population are all stochastic processes, and the abundance of non-local, colonizing recruits is drawn from a bimodal distribution with two means separated by an order of magnitude to represent colonization variability across El Niño and non-El Niño years. The model uses a monthly time step from $t$ to $t+1$, and the dynamics depend upon the month, $M(t)$ due to seasonal variation in population processes. More detail can be found in the Supplemental Information.

In addition to process variation (i.e., stochasticity in rates of population growth, colonization, removal), the state dynamics, $P$, also account for parametric uncertainty. The Bayesian posterior estimated by \cite{keller2026integrated}, $\sigma$, is propagated into the RL environment such that each simulation episode of the environment uses posterior sample $\sigma^i$ (i.e., $P^i(\textbf{s}_{t+1}|\textbf{s}_t,a_t, M(t), \sigma^i)$) (Table S1).

\subsubsection{Action space}

We assume a decision maker chooses the action, $a_t$, or number of traps to set in month $M(t)$ based on observation $o_{t-1}$. The decision maker has two trap types to use, Minnow and Fukui traps ($a_t=\{a_M, a_F\}_t$), with different size-selective removal rates (Figure S1). The Minnow trap removal rate follow a bell-shaped curve as a function of size, as the mesh size is smaller than the maximum crab size, and Fukui trap removal rate follow a logistic curve as a function of size (Figure 1). Neither trap type catches crabs with a carapace width smaller than $\approx 30$ mm. The available actions range continuously from 0 to 3000 traps for both $a_M$ and $a_F$. 

\subsubsection{Observation space}

To understand the appropriate observation complexity for optimal control in green crab management, we consider a set of four observation types with different amounts and types of information. These observations are all based on $h_{t,y}$, the count of harvested crabs of size $y$. The process by which the count of harvested crabs, $h_{t,y}$, is generated from the size-structured abundance, $\textbf{s}_t$ and removal action, $a_t$ (i.e., $P(\textbf{h}_t | a_t, \textbf{s}_t)$) is further described in the Supplemental Information.

The first observation type, $O_1$, is the one-dimensional catch per unit effort (CPUE) of harvested crabs of all size classes $O_1 = \frac{\sum_yh_y}{a_{M}+a_{F}}$. The second observation type, $O^T_1$, includes both the one-dimensional CPUE of harvested crabs and the month associated with $t$, $O^T_1 = \{\frac{\sum_yh_y}{a_M+a_F}, M(t)\}$. The third observation type, $O^T_2$, includes the one-dimensional CPUE of harvested crabs, mean biomass of harvested crabs, and the month associated with $t$, $O^T_2 = \{\frac{\sum_yh_y}{a_M+a_F}, B(\textbf{h}),M(t)\}$. The function relating mean biomass to the size-structured count of removed crabs, $B(\textbf{h})$, can be found in the supplemental information. Finally, the fourth observation type, $O^T_{22}$, includes the 22-dimensional, size-structured CPUE of harvested crabs and the month associated with $t$, $O^T_{22} = \{\frac{h_y}{a_M+a_F}, M(t)\}$.

\subsubsection{Reward function}

Due to the green crab's long distance dispersal and high potential for recolonization after removal, eradication of green crab is typically not considered achievable \parencite{tummon2024rebound}. Instead, the goal of ``functional eradication" has emerged as a pragmatic objective, where the invader population is suppressed below levels that cause unacceptable ecological effects, while balancing the need to minimize removal costs \parencite{green2021functional}.

We therefore formulate the reward function, $r(\textbf{s}_t, a_t)$, as the sum of the reward associated with ecological change, $r^E(\textbf{s}_t)$, and the reward associated with removal cost, $r^C(a_t)$, with higher values corresponding to more desirable outcomes:

\begin{equation}
r(\textbf{s}_t, a_t) = r^E(\textbf{s}_t) + r^C(a_t)
\end{equation}

The shape of the relationship between biomass and ecological impact, $r^E(B(\textbf{s}_t))$, is likely nonlinear and depends upon the impacted resource \parencite{bradley2019disentangling, grosholz2000impacts}. Here we consider a sigmoidal relationship between biomass and ecological impact, which may be consistent with the effect of green crab on eelgrass shoot density, where the impact of green crab foraging behavior is minimal until a threshold at which ecological integrity declines \parencite{davis1998quantifying}:

\begin{equation}
r^E(\textbf{s}_t) = \frac{c^\alpha}{1 - \text{exp}(c^\gamma \times(B(\textbf{s}_t) /A-c^\beta))}
\end{equation}

Here, $A$ is the area of the habitat, such that the reward can be interpreted as a function of green crab density.

Reward also scales linearly as a function of removal effort:

\begin{equation}
r^C(a_t) = c^\phi\times(a_t^M + a_t^F)
\end{equation}

Parameter values for the above reward functions are chosen to reflect a decision maker who values minimizing ecological change more than minimizing the cost of removal, such that the minimum possible value for $r^E(\textbf{s}_t)$ (-0.265 - 0) is 1.65x greater than the minimum possible value for $r^C(a_t)$ (-0.16 - 0). Values for reward function parameters can be found in Table S3.

\subsection{Optimizing removal policies}

Here we describe methods used to calculate or approximate the optimal policy function, $\pi^*: O \rightarrow A$ that maps an optimal action to an observation.

\subsubsection{Reinforcement learning-based policies}

The reinforcement learning-based policies use a neural network to approximate an optimal policy function, $\pi^*_R$, and define the relationship between the observation, $o_t$, and action $a_t$:

\begin{equation}
a_t = f_{\theta}(o_t)
\end{equation}

where $f_\theta$ is the neural network with parameters $\theta$. Reinforcement learning was used to optimize four different observation types of varying observation type and complexity, $O_1$, $O_1^T$, $O^2_2$, and $O_{22}^T$, and a different set of parameters, $\theta$, were optimized for each observation type. 

Optimizing these neural network weights, $\theta$, involves \textit{training} the agent, a process where the agent collects data on interactions with the environment and learns actions to encourage or avoid, given an observation and the reward it receives \parencite{lapeyrolerie2022deep}. Multiple algorithms exist for training RL agents. We trained RL agents for each observation type with three algorithms: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \parencite{schulman2017ppo}, Truncated Quantile Critic (TQC) \parencite{kuznetsov2020tqc}, and Twin-delayed Deep Deterministic (TD3) \parencite{fujimoto2018addressing}. For each observation type and algorithm combination, ten replicate agents were trained for 10,000,000 timesteps with 100 time steps per episode (100,000 episodes). Each episode used a separate draw from the posterior, $\sigma^i$, from \cite{keller2026integrated} such that parametric uncertainty is propagated and the agent is interacting with an environment that uses posterior sample $\sigma^i$ (i.e., $P^i(\textbf{s}_{t+1}|\textbf{s}_t,a_t, M(t), \sigma^i)$). For implementation, we used the Stable Baselines library in Python \parencite{stable-baselines3}. Details are provided in open-source companion code.

\subsubsection{Optimal constant policy}

To build a reference point for understanding the performance of RL-based policies, we calculated a constant, observation-invariant policy, $\pi^*_C$, that applies the same action at every time step $t$. We used Bayesian optimization with a Gaussian process estimator to find the action, $a = \{a_M, a_F\}$ that maximized the cumulative reward across 200 episodes (100 time steps per episode) \parencite{skopt}.

\subsection{Policy evaluation}

To enable interpretation of the RL policies and neural network weights, $\theta$, we simulated $n = 500$ episodes of 100 time steps of the environment and applied RL policy, $\pi^*_R$ (i.e., action $a_t$ associated with an observation $o_t$), for each of ten replicate trained RL agents for each algorithm/observation type combination. Hereafter, "policy evaluation" refers to application of a policy during simulated episodes of the environment. We recorded the actions, observations, and state at each time step, as well as the cumulative reward at the end of each episode at $t = 100$. To allow comparison across algorithms and observation types, we used the same draws from the stochastic elements of the environment (i.e., environmental stochasticity during density-dependent recruitment, abundance of colonizing non-local recruits, etc) and the same sequence of parameter values sampled from the \cite{keller2026integrated} posterior distribution, $\sigma$.

\subsection{Generating actionable policies}

To increase the interpretability and utility of the RL-based policies in management processes, we used a clustering approach to convert the continuous, neural network-based policy, $\pi^*_R$, to a discrete policy, $\pi^*_D$. This discrete, clustered policy was developed with simulated data (continuous actions, $a_t = \{a_M, a_F\}_t$, associated with continuous observations, $o_t$) from policy evaluations with the RL agent replicate, algorithm, and observation type with the highest mean cumulative reward. The simulated data, $\{a_M, a_F, o\}_t$, were standardized and clustered using $k$-means clustering with the number of clusters $k$ specified \textit{a priori} to reflect the number of qualitatively distinct action regimes evident in the simulated data. The discrete policy, $\pi^*_D$, maps a continuous observation to a discrete action, $\pi^*_D: O \rightarrow a^D = \{a_M, a_F\}^D$, using the cluster centroid closest in Euclidean space to the continuous observation.

To compare the discrete policy, $\pi^*_D$, to the RL-based policy, $\pi^*_R$, and constant action policy, $\pi^*_C$, we simulated $n = 500$ episodes of 100 time steps of the environment and applied the discrete policy. We recorded the actions, observations, and state at each time step, as well as the cumulative reward at the end of each episode at $t =100$. We then visualized the discrete policy, $\pi^*_D: O \rightarrow a^D$, using a convex hull around the observations, $O$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Reinforcement learning environment}

We developed a reinforcement learning environment for an agent to interact with and learn an optimal policy, $\pi^*_R$, that maps an action to an observation. The environment reflects size-structured European green crab population dynamics and a size-structured observation process and is conceptually equivalent to a combination of the state dynamics ($P(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t, M(t))$) and observation function ($Z(o_{t+1}|a_t, s_{t+1}$) in a partially observable Markov decision process (Figure 1).

The environment simulates an episode, or 100 time steps of the population dynamics and observation process. Across the episode, the total crab abundance reaches an equilibrium near carrying capacity, yet stochasticity in inter-annual colonization rates leads to variable abundance across episodes (Figure 2A). The mean size of crabs increases as the crabs grow, with the abundance of crabs >65 mm increasing within each year (months 4-10) (Figure 2A). The opportunity for crabs to be observed changes within the year as the crabs grow into trappable size classes (Figure 2). The size structure of observed crabs (Figure 2B, bottom panel) is therefore not proportionate to the true size structure of crabs (Figure 2A, top panel).

\subsection{Right-sizing model complexity}

To find the observation complexity that yields the highest-performing reinforcement learning policy, we created four separate environments that produced different observation types of increasing complexity, $O_1$, $O_1^T$, $O_2^T$, and $O_{22}^T$. We trained RL agents using three different algorithms on each observation type. For each observation type-algorithm combination, ten replicate agents were trained to account for stochasticity in the training process. We then compared the cumulative reward across 500 simulated episodes (i.e., policy evaluations) by applying the RL-based policy, $\pi^*_R$ for each observation type-algorithm combination and trained RL agent replicate. 

The highest performing observation type for all RL algorithms was $O_2^T$ (Figure 3; Figure S3; Table 1). With this medium-complexity observation type, the RL agent observed the month ($M(t)$), catch per unit effort (CPUE) across all crab sizes, and the mean biomass of caught crabs. The RL-based policy, $\pi^*_R$ with all observation types out-performed the constant action policy, $\pi^*_C$, that applies the same action at every time step, invariant of the observation (Table 1). Yet the RL-based policy with the low-complexity observation types ($O_1$ and $O_1^T$) that did not contain any information about crab size only marginally out-performed the constant action policy (Figure 3; Table 1). The highest performing RL algorithm with the $O_2^T$ observation was the Truncated Quantile Critic algorithm (TQC), although the Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm (PPO) was consistently high performing across all observation types (Figure S3; Table S4). The mean cumulative reward varied across the ten replicate trained RL agents for each observation type-algorithm combination (Table 1). Both the low complexity observation type ($O_1$) and TD3 RL algorithm produced particularly high between-replicate variance (Table 1; Table S4).

\subsection{Reinforcement learning-based policies}

The highest-performing RL-based policy using the medium-complexity observation type ($O^T_2$) and the TQC algorithm relates the optimal removal effort at $t$ ($a_t = {a_M, a_F}_t$) to observations of catch per unit effort (CPUE) and mean biomass at $t-1$ for each month $M(t)$ throughout the trapping season (April-October) (Figure 4). To understand the neural network weights, $\theta$, associated with the trained RL agent, we simulated 500 episodes of 100 time steps of the environment and applied RL policy, $\pi^*_R$ (i.e., action $a_t$ associated with an observation $o_t$). The simulated actions and observations demonstrate that the optimal action varies strongly over time as the size structure of the crab population changes (Figure 4). For Minnow traps ($a_M$) that target medium-sized crabs (Figure S1), very few traps should be set in the months of April-August, and the optimal trapping intensity depends primarily on the catch per unit effort (CPUE) in the months of September and October (Figure 4). For Fukui traps ($a_F$) that target medium- and large-sized crabs (Figure S1), the optimal trapping intensity varies across all three observation dimensions: month ($M(t)$), catch per unit effort (CPUE), and mean biomass of caught crabs (Figure 4).

\subsection{Actionable, reinforcement learning-informed policies}

We used $k$-means clustering to generate a discrete, simplified policy, $\pi^*_D$, informed by the continuous, reinforcement learning-based policy (Figure 5A; Figure S4). This simplified policy takes a continuous observation ($O^T_2$), finds the closest cluster centroid in Euclidean space, and maps the continuous observation to a discrete action, $a^D$ (Figure 5A). Though simpler than the RL-based policy, this discrete, actionable policy does not decline in performance. Applying the discrete, cluster-based policy during policy evaluations produces a cumulative reward nearly equal in performance to the RL-based policy (Figure 5B; Table 1).
